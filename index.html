How the bottles move

Each bottle rides the ocean's surface currents. The currents come from HYCOM, the Hybrid Coordinate Ocean Model run by the US Navy and its partners, which blends satellite and buoy measurements with ocean physics. I averaged its surface currents for each calendar month over five years (September 2019 to August 2024) on a grid of a quarter of a degree, about 28 km at the equator.

Every simulated day, a bottle moves with the current where it sits, blended between months as the seasons turn. Real oceans are full of eddies, swirls tens of kilometres wide that monthly averages smooth away, so each bottle also takes a small random step every day. That is why bottles dropped at the same spot drift apart. A bottle that reaches land has washed up.

The bottles ride the water only. Real floating objects also get pushed by the wind, and the more they stick out of the water the more it pushes them, so treat these paths as a picture of where the sea itself would carry them.
